# Email Framework — Email X / Variable Y

**Email X** = the fixed skeleton (stays ~constant across prospects). **Variable Y** = the fields you fill in per prospect.

## Email X — 5 parts
1. **Opening** — no generic warm-up small talk. "Warm-ups are used by people who didn't do real work."
2. **Observation** — one specific, verifiable friction. Names the page. Proves you looked without sounding like an attack — you're pointing out friction, not judging competence.
3. **Mechanics** — explained in *pattern* language, never a metric claim. No numbers, no "$X/month lost," no conversion-rate claims. Chain: one clean change → visitor hesitates at that step → leaves → attach hesitation to a recognizable, plausible reason.
4. **Offer of free value** — created *after* they reply, not before. Small in scope (one friction only), fast to judge, safe to decline. This is risk reversal, not politeness.
5. **Micro-yes CTA** — "Not — are you free?" style. Requires no planning, no commitment. Never a second CTA — extra CTAs create friction and kill replies.

**What ruins it:** over-explaining mechanics, adding multiple issues, sounding like an agency pitch (services/teams/packages/results talk), asking for a call in email #1, hype language ("game changer," "massive," "explode," "revolutionary").

## Variable Y — 8 fields (fill per prospect)
Everything in brackets must be observable or conditional — sound accurate, not personal. You're describing what any careful observer would notice, not claiming intimate knowledge of their business.

1. **First name** — source: LinkedIn, business directories, press/about pages. Test: "Would you bet money it's correct?" If not, leave it out.
2. **Company name** — public brand name, no unneeded legal suffixes (no "LLC/Inc") unless that's genuinely how they present.
3. **Page** — short page name (Home/Menu/About etc.)
4. **Website_problem** — one line, from vision issues (if broken) or a business gap found via search (if the site is fine)
5. **Recognizable_reason** — why a visitor notices this gap; a behavior, not a theory
6. **Consequence_mechanics** — the chain reaction, sourced truthfully: friction → hesitation → (pattern language) → fewer leads/bookings/purchases. Test: "Is the chain a chain, not a claim?"
7. **Mechanism** — the choke point closest to money
8. **Subject_label** — 2–5 words, ties to the micro-yes inside Email X

## Pre-send checklist (10-second test)
1. Can you verify the issue in 10 seconds?
2. Is it one friction on one page?
3. Is the recognizable reason a behavior, not a theory?
4. Is the consequence mechanic a chain, not a claim?
5. Does the subject line match the micro-yes inside Email X?

If it passes, send. If it fails, fix the variables — not the structure. If you can't find a real friction, don't force one; forcing weak frictions trains you to write spam.

## Follow-ups
Follow-ups don't get new icebreakers. They focus on: whether they saw the previous email, the business result, the demo, a low-pressure next step, a quick yes/no.

### Follow-up #2 — quality checklist
Same issue as Outreach #1. Recognizable reason simpler/plainer than last time.
Consequence mechanic even more direct. **Shorter than Outreach #1, nothing new added.**
If any check fails, don't send. If you can't keep it shorter, you're compensating for
weak clarity — fix that by choosing a better friction, not by writing more. If a reply
says "stop," don't argue — remove the lead and move on.

### Follow-up #3 — the last useful nudge
Reinforces time safety and sets a clean boundary without sounding emotional. Proves this
isn't a drawn-out process — a 3-minute decision. **The shorter your emails get across
touches, the more confident you appear.**

**Rules:**
1. Must not re-explain mechanics.
2. Must reduce everything to one friction and one outcome, nothing more.
3. Must include a boundary signaling this is the last nudge.
4. Must keep the same permission-based micro-yes.
5. Must not mention meetings, calls, pricing, packaging, or implementation.

**Structure, in 4 blocks:**
1. Mark it as the last useful nudge — one sentence. Not giving up; just not becoming
   inbox noise. Don't ask if they saw the earlier emails.
2. Restate the offer as a focused preview, in the smallest possible way — one friction,
   one outcome.
3. Scope boundary creates safety — if you can't state scope in one sentence, it's too
   big. Restate how fast it is to judge (minutes, not a project).
4. Repeat the same micro-yes and stop. No re-explaining, no new CTA. State plainly:
   "If I don't hear back, I'll stop nudging."

## Follow-up #4 — the final binary email (day 7, closes the sequence)
The shortest email in the whole sequence. No restating the issue, no value stack, no
"what we do" language. The ask is binary and permission-based. No secondary CTA, no
calendar link. Must feel safe to forward internally.

**3 blocks:**
1. Close the loop — one short, neutral line. Use first name only if used in earlier
   touches.
2. Restate the offer in plain terms — one line, no extra explanation.
3. The binary question — one clean yes/no. No "either way," no extra sentence that
   reopens the thread.

Variable Y here is the smallest set in the whole sequence: **first name, company name,
the free value** — nothing else. If you get no reply after day 7, end the thread — don't
keep nudging past it. If the sequence isn't converting, fix the earlier emails (friction
choice, mechanics) on the next cycle — don't patch it in the final email.

## Subject line rules
The subject line is not where you sell — it's a label on a business message. Your only
job is to earn one click. It must read like a message from a vendor partner or someone
inside the business, not like outreach.

**Rules:**
1. Keep it short — 3–4 words. If you can't say it that short without losing meaning,
   tighten it.
2. Plain business voice — like something a real person typed quickly.
3. Must preview the action inside the email, or it feels like bait.
4. Use names only when natural — forced first-name usage looks automated and can hurt
   deliverability.
5. Use numbers only when they increase clarity, never to look impressive.
6. No hype language ("game changer," "massive").
7. Avoid campaign signals: over-capitalization, sales adjectives, spam-shaped questions,
   weird spacing/punctuation.

**Subject and CTA are a pair** — if the CTA is permission-based, the subject previews
permission. The subject is a promise the email's first line must fulfill.

**Tests before sending:**
- Would this look normal from a vendor you already work with?
- Would this look normal from someone inside the company?
- Does it belong in a business email, or does it read as marketing?
- The "strip the name" test: remove the recipient's name — does it still feel
  one-to-one? If it falls apart, it was relying on fake personalization.

If opens are low, it's deliverability, a subject that reads as outreach, or bad
targeting — never fix low opens by escalating hype.

## Yes Flow — what to do the moment they reply yes
A "yes" is a window, not a guarantee — it decays fast. Respond fast; speed here is
competence, not desperation.

**Classify the yes first:**
1. **Permission-yes** — gave permission to send the free value.
2. **Curious-yes** — leaning in, asking questions, hasn't seen proof yet.
3. **Call-yes** — explicitly asked for a call or offered time.

**Then respond:**
1. Confirm and deliver cleanly — no extra selling, no new claims.
2. Keep it view-only, low friction — no attachments, logins, access requests, or forms.
   One clean link, no shorteners, no multiple links.
3. Give one instruction for what to look at — confused prospects stop engaging.
4. Ask one qualifying question that feels natural — confirms the workflow is live/worth
   pursuing, confirms the pain, sets up the next step without pressure.
5. Don't ask for a call until it's earned (they've seen the value or confirmed the pain
   is real) — a call isn't a reward for replying, it's a scope-and-risk meeting.
6. Preserve optionality — stay on one workflow; multiple asks create implementation fear.
7. Set boundaries and protect trust — if you said view-only, it stays view-only.

**Mistakes:** sending the free value late (kills momentum), adding new claims, sending
messy/multiple links, sending multiple free values at once, asking for a call
immediately, asking more than one qualifying question, sounding excited (excitement
reads as sales pressure — calm reads as competence).

**If they viewed it but didn't respond:** don't panic — follow up with a new
angle/reason, reduce review time, clarify what to look at, or propose a short call with
a clear purpose. Don't start discovery over email.
