# The Decision Call — Full Framework

This is the actual sales-call system. Skills are taught out of live-call order (you don't
learn a fight by fighting on day one), but here is the **real, assembled call flow** —
use this order when running a call.

## The End Game
You reach out first, you earn the conversation, then you run the decision-maker call.
A **win** = you uncover the real pain that costs them (confirmed), you confirm the
decision-maker is real and qualified, you frame the outcome so they can justify it
internally, and you secure a clear next step. Standard: not pitching early, not
desperation, not rambling — just control.

## The 13-step call flow

1. **Call Control — first 60 seconds.** If you don't control the opening, you don't
   control the call.
   - Confirm time first: "We've got 15 minutes — sound fair?" Structure, not an open-ended ramble.
   - State the agenda plainly: current setup, what it's costing, what's recommended, next steps.
   - Vendor vs. peer framing: vendors ask permission to exist; peers set the process.
   - **Two openers to avoid:** the friendly waffle (small talk that delays business) and
     the instant pitch (triggers the buyer's defense system — nobody wants a solution
     before they feel their problem has been seen).
   - One-line rule: if you haven't diagnosed yet, your opening is not for explanation.

2. **Diagnose** — find the real problem under the surface symptoms. Reflect back what
   you heard before moving on ("let me make sure I get this right..."). Being understood
   is the gateway to being sold.

3. **Quantify** — convert the problem into cost across 4 lanes: time, money, risk,
   reputation. See offer-blueprint.md for the full quantification method (frequency →
   magnitude → period total → confirm back).

4. **Confirm** — say the number back out loud and get agreement before moving on.

5. **Qualify — is it a fit?** Three gates:
   - **Priority** — "On a scale of 1 to 10, how urgent is this?"
   - **Ability to buy** — "Is budget already allocated for solving this? What's the
     normal approval path?" (Ability = money AND attention/bandwidth.)
   - **Timeline** — "When do you want this solved by? If we did nothing for the next
     30 days, what happens?" **If there's no timeline, it's a future maybe, not a deal.**
   - Summarize and confirm out loud: "Based on what you said, this is a priority because
     the cost is roughly [$X], and [budget/decision path] is [Y] — is that accurate?"

6. **Decision Map** — now that the pain is clear, map who signs off (MPA), what approval
   exists, what kills deals internally.

7. **Value Frame** — decision-makers buy outcomes, not technology. Outcome first,
   mechanism second, tool talk last (if ever). 3-part script:
   1. "Based on what you told me, the core issue is [X] — time/money/risk/reputation."
   2. "The outcome we want is [a measurable result in a time frame]."
   3. "We [mechanism in plain English]." — then **stop talking.** The pause does the work.
   - Never use tool names ("LLM agent," "API integration") — jargon repeated back wrong
     becomes a perceived risk. No engineering lectures before commitment.

8. **The Challenge Skill** — used throughout the call, not a single step. Any time the
   prospect drifts, reframe calmly.

9. **Objection Prevention** — throughout the call, surface constraints and decision
   criteria early, on your terms, before they become objections. Magic prevention line:
   give them a safe moment to be honest early.

   **The 3-line close (prevents objections before they start):**
   1. "Based on what you've said, if we can deliver [outcome] within [time frame]
      whilst respecting [constraint]..."
   2. "What could stop this?"
   3. "Is there anything you're unsure about that would make you hesitate?"

10. **Objection Handling** — even with perfect prevention, objections still appear
    (missing info, felt risk, or not the real decision-maker). Be calm and surgical.
    Surface objections ("too expensive," "already have a tool") are rarely the real
    reason — usually fear of change underneath.

    **Framework: Clarify → Isolate → Reframe → Confirm.**
    1. Clarify — turn fog into something real: "When you say it's too expensive, do
       you mean A or B?"
    2. Isolate — is this the *actual* main blocker? "Is this the main blocker?"
    3. Reframe — change the meaning, not the fact. Don't argue the objection, reposition it.
    4. Confirm — "Does that address it? Fair?" If not, loop back to clarify.

    **Worked examples:**
    - *"It's too expensive."* → Clarify: relative to budget, or perceived value? →
      Isolate: is budget still the blocker if value is clear and risk is low? →
      Reframe: "You're leaking roughly [$X] — that's why we start with a contained
      pilot." → Confirm: "Does this remove the price concern?"
    - *"We don't have the time right now."* → Clarify what "time" means (overloaded vs.
      don't want another project) → Reframe: "The point of this is to give you time
      back — inputs are light, outcomes are measurable. If we keep inputs to [X], we
      handle the rest." → Confirm: "Can we take the next step?"
    - *"We already have a tool for that."* → Reframe: "What outcome was that tool
      meant to deliver? If it's not delivering it, would you be open to a better path?
      We're not replacing tools for fun — we deliver the outcome without disrupting
      what you use."
    - *"I need to think about it."* → Clarify: priority, risk, or missing info — which
      one? → Reframe: "What would you need to see to make it an obvious yes or no? If
      that cost is happening monthly, the clean move is a small diagnostic now."

    **Critical execution note:** after you reframe, stop talking. Over-talking after a
    reframe signals insecurity. Don't loop clarify→isolate→reframe→confirm more than
    once — resolve and move on.

    **Clean exit line when it genuinely isn't a fit:** "It sounds like this isn't a
    priority right now. If anything changes, hit me up and we can revisit."

11. **Commitment Engineering** — a purpose-driven conversation that ends with a locked
    next step, not "let's stay in touch." Decision-makers avoid committing when the next
    step feels risky, scope is unclear, or the outcome feels uncertain. Fix: **sell the
    next logical step, not the whole deal.**

    **The 3-step commitment ladder:** Audit/Diagnostic → Pilot → Rollout.
    - Audit/Diagnostic — use when they need a plan, multiple stakeholders, or data needs
      mapping before you know what to do and in what order.
    - Pilot — use when they want results and just need the operation moving before
      scaling. Outcome: "we prove the outcome."
    - Rollout — full commitment once the pilot has proven the outcome.

12. **Proposal Discipline** — only if necessary, and **never without a booked review slot.**
    - Title the proposal by outcome, not service — brain reads value first.
    - One page: the breakdown point, quantified cost, why now.
    - **Scope box with clear inclusions/exclusions** — most price comparison happens
      when scope is ambiguous; clarity shuts down negotiation.
    - Risk-control ladder (pilot before rollout) — signals real process.
    - A one-line decision-criteria filter: "This makes sense if you care about X and
      want Y by Z."
    - Must include: next step, scope, timeline, owner inputs (what you provide, what
      they provide), investment (price + payment terms), decision step.
    - Send with a message: "Sent — don't review it alone, let's book time to align and
      decide — still on for [date]?" Never let a proposal be reviewed in isolation.
    - **"No proposal without a calendar."**
    - Two deflections: *"Just send me pricing"* → "Two people can say 'website' and
      mean different things — let me send outcome, scope, and investment so it's
      clean," then book the follow-up. *"We're just getting a few quotes"* → "Makes
      sense — tell me what they're quoting and I'll tell you what's missing."

13. **Post-Call Follow-Through** — most deals die *after* the call, not on it. Book a
    fixed slot (e.g. "Tuesday 11am"), not something loose. Send a recap right after the
    call, as one short forwardable paragraph, bullet points:
    1. Trigger / why now
    2. The diagnosed problem (not just the surface symptom)
    3. Quantified cost (at least one number, tied to risk/reputation where relevant)
    4. Outcome + timeframe
    5. Next step, date and time
    6. Ownership list

14. **Exit Rules** — kill bad deals cleanly. If no priority, no timeline, or no ability
    → exit the deal. Disqualify politely with status, leave the door open: "When X is
    true, let's revisit." Protects your time and keeps the pipeline clean.

## The 15-Second Offer Test
If you can't say your offer in 15 seconds without notes, you don't own it. Must include
only 4 components: **outcome, mechanism, differentiator, next step** — nothing else. No
steps, no tools, no proof stacking, no pricing. This is your default operating state for
describing the offer in a doorway, not a rehearsed pitch.

Worked example: "We help [buyer] go from [pain] to [outcome] without sacrificing quality
for quantity. Most people use basic automations and hope someone follows up. We do this
through an automated triage system that qualifies intent immediately and routes without
delay. This works because humans are slow and automations usually respond too late. It
runs inside your existing setup and fixes response speed. Solutions like this typically
start at $[X] depending on scope. Next step: we secure the investment and begin
execution — within [X] days we do a walkthrough."

## Pricing Gravity
Pricing is neurological, not logical — hesitation transfers to the buyer if it's not
resolved before the conversation. Ignore market noise / competitor pricing — it doesn't
decide your price.

**4-step system:**
1. Calculate your real operating cost to deliver cleanly (not what you want to make).
2. **Multiply by 3 — this is your pricing floor.** Absolute minimum. Never go below it,
   not to be nice, not for experience, not to close faster.
3. **Install the walkaway rule.** Decide in advance: if a buyer won't meet your floor,
   disengage calmly — no arguing, no justifying. They either submit to your frame or
   reject it — both fine, now you know if they were the right fit.
4. **Outcome escalation** — price rises only because the outcome gets heavier, never
   because you want more. 4 levers: **speed** (faster delivery = higher value), **risk
   absorbed** (guarantees transfer risk to you = buyer pays more), **scope** (more
   systems touched, less buyer effort = higher price), **certainty** (tighter process,
   fewer variables = buyers pay to remove uncertainty).

**Never decrease price** because a buyer hesitates or you want the deal — emotional
pricing destroys authority. Price only moves up as outcomes get heavier, never down
under pressure.

## Decision Map — the 4 boxes (deeper detail)
"Besides you, who signs off?"

1. **Economic buyer** — who controls the budget, who can actually say yes. Titles lie —
   ask directly: "When this gets approved, whose name is on the spend?" / "If we decide
   to move, who signs off?" / "Who has final authority on this?"
2. **Influencers** — who can bless or kill a deal even without being on the call.
   "Who else would weigh in on this?" / "Would [role] have concerns?" / "If someone says
   no, who is that usually?"
3. **Process** — budget, legal, or operations approval steps. "What's the normal process
   for approving something like this?" / "Does this require budget, legal, or ops
   sign-off?" / "What would need to be true for you to greenlight this quickly?"
4. **Deal killers** — what commonly stops similar deals. "What usually stops things like
   this from going ahead?" / "What made you say no to something similar before?"

If you're speaking to a messenger, not the buyer: "If this makes sense, what's the
fastest path to get this in front of the person who approves it?"

## Diagnose — the 5-layer discovery flow (full script)
Most of the time, what they say first is a symptom, not the real problem. Never pitch
early. Five layers, each with its own questions:

1. **Surface** — what's happening. "What's happening right now?" / "What made you take
   this call?" / "What would you like to be different?"
2. **Mechanism** — where it breaks. "Walk me through what happens start to finish."
   "Where does it break most often?" / "What part feels messy?" / "When it goes wrong,
   what's usually the cause?"
3. **Impact** — what it costs. "What does that cost you in a normal week?" / "If nothing
   changes, what happens in the next 90 days?" / "How does this affect revenue, time, and
   risk or reputation?" / "What's the hidden cost people don't see?"
4. **Attempts** — what they've tried. "What have you tried so far?" / "What worked a
   little, or didn't?" / "Why do you think it hasn't been solved?" / "What are you
   worried will happen if it happens again?"
5. **Priority** — why now. "Why is this important to fix now?" / "Where does this sit
   compared to other priorities?" / "What happens if we do nothing?" / "What would make
   this a clear win for you?"

**Two golden questions to get to the truth (use gently):** "Tell me more about that?"
and "What's the real reason?"

**Never:** pitch, or ask random unrelated questions. **Summarize at the end:** symptom,
breakdown point, cost, what they've tried, why it matters now.

**Baseline script to adapt:** "What prompted this call? What's happening right now?
Walk me through your process from start to finish. Where does it break most often? What
does that cost you in a normal week? What have you tried so far? Why do you think it
hasn't been solved yet? Why is this important to fix now? What would a clear win look
like in the next 30 days?"

## The Challenge Skill — 5 types, with scripts
Push back calmly, reframe, hold boundaries. Order: anchor the relationship (validate —
"totally fair," "I hear you," "good question") → ask permission to direct ("Can I be
direct?" / "Let me push back for a second") → reframe with a reason → offer a better path.

1. **False problem** — *"We need more leads."* → "Maybe, but leads aren't usually the
   problem — they're the visible symptom. Before we chase volume, I'd like to see where
   leads are leaking. Otherwise you'll pay twice."
2. **Speed/cheap pressure** — *"We just want something quick and cheap."* → "If cheap is
   the priority, you'll get quick and cheap outcomes. Do you want a system that looks
   good, or one that produces results? We can run a contained pilot fast, but we don't
   cut corners that protect the outcome."
3. **Tool obsession** — *"Are you using ChatGPT or Claude? What stack?"* → "Tool choice
   doesn't matter to the result, outcomes do. If we hit the outcome, the tool is
   invisible. Let's define the outcome and constraints first, then pick the simplest
   mechanism."
4. **Scope creep** — *"Can you add X, Y, and Z?"* → "We can, but that changes scope — and
   if scope expands, timeline and cost can too. Cleanest path: deliver the core outcome
   first, then expand."
5. **Soft rejection** — *"Let me think about it."* → "When someone tells me that, it
   usually means one of three things: the priority isn't high, the path isn't clear, or
   there's a concern that hasn't surfaced yet. Which one is it?"

**Tone:** slow voice, short sentences, direct language, no fluff, no need to win — just
clarify. Never emotional.

## Objection Prevention (distinct from Objection Handling below)
Surface constraints and decision criteria *before* they harden into objections —
prevention happens throughout the call, not as a single step.

**Framework: Label → Clarify → De-risk → Confirm.**
Worked example (budget): "Okay, budget constraint — that's the label. Is it not
allocated, or can't be justified? — that's clarifying it. We can run a diagnostic or
pilot to create certainty before rolling anything out — that de-risks it. If the pilot
proves X, is the budget still a blocker? — that confirms it's resolved."

Ask directly: "What would you need to see or believe for this to be an obvious yes?"
"What could stop this from happening even if it made sense to both of us?" Categories
objections usually fall into: money, time, risk, control, past pain, internal blocker.

## Commitment Engineering — deciding which rung of the ladder
- Are they clear on the problem already? Yes → pilot or rollout. No → audit.
- Do they have fear, low trust, or internal blockers? Yes → push for a pilot (audit if
  even that's too much). No → go straight to rollout.
- Is the outcome measurable quickly? Yes → pilot. No → audit/diagnostic.
- Is there a clear decision-maker? No → audit/diagnostic. Yes → pilot or rollout.

**Locking it in:** confirm agreement → define what happens next ("in that pilot, we'll
do X to produce Y within Z days") → assign ownership explicitly ("on my side I will...,
on your side I'll need...") → book the calendar right then, give two concrete time
options → confirm ("once that's booked, I'll send a short recap so your team can align").

**Common commitment killers:**
- *"Send me something first."* → "Happy to — what I don't want is you reading a doc and
  going nowhere. Let's book a 15-minute slot now, and I'll send a summary to support that."
- *"I need to discuss this with someone else."* → "Makes sense — let's book a short call
  with them so I can answer directly, rather than it becoming a game of retelling."

**Memorize: the call isn't complete until the next step is booked.**

## Exit Rules — specific scripts per trigger
Kill bad deals cleanly: no priority, no timeline, no ability to buy, no respect for
process, price-only mindset, misalignment, high friction early.

**Ladder:** clarify reality ("just so I'm accurate, where does this sit on your priority
list?") → offer a clean path ("if it's a priority, the next step is X, let's lock it in")
→ if they won't commit, disqualify ("no worries, it sounds like this isn't live enough
to act on right now") → leave the door open ("if that changes, reach out and we'll pick
it back up").

- **Not a priority:** "Totally fair — from what I'm hearing this isn't a priority right
  now. I don't want to take your time if it's not live. If it becomes urgent, message me."
- **No timeline:** "Makes sense — without a timeline it's hard to do this properly.
  Let's pause it. Feel free to call when you're ready to move."
- **No authority:** "Got it — sounds like someone else would need to sign off. Clean next
  step is we get them on a short call. If that's not possible, we pause until it is."
- **Price only:** "If you're choosing purely on lowest price, I'm not the best fit. We
  focus on outcome and control — I don't want to win a deal if I can't win on quality."
- **Ghosting:** "I'm going to close this out for now so it doesn't sit open. If you want
  to restart, just reply with a time that works and I'll pick it up from there."
