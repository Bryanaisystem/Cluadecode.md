# Offer Blueprint — Pain Point Framework

Sequence: pick the buyer → choose one pain → measurable outcome → mechanism → scope → proof → price → package → 15-second test.

## Pick one buyer
Offers get bought by a specific person inside the business who holds the power to say yes, not by "the business." The Lead Finder Machine work already extracts buyers, not just businesses — keep that person in mind for every piece of the offer.

## Choose one pain
A pain only counts if it costs something in one of 4 lanes. If you can't name the cost, it's a vague "consultant word," not a real pain.

1. **Time** — "leads aren't followed up fast enough" → "we're drowning, can't keep up"
2. **Money** — missed leads, slow response losing deals, wasted paid traffic → "we're leaking money." Two types: leakage (revenue already slipping away) vs. hard cost (staff time/external spend)
3. **Risk** — compliance issues, inconsistent handling → "one mistake could cost us"
4. **Reputation** — broken websites, bad handoffs → lost trust, bad reviews

Always translate the technical pain into the plain-English cost the owner actually feels — that translation is what turns a tool into an offer.

## Quantify the pain
Structure: get them to state what's happening → convert to frequency ("how often?") → convert to magnitude (minutes/hours/dollars/deals) → do the math for a period total → confirm back ("is that fair, or am I missing something?").

- Time: "how many hours a week does this eat? What should that person be doing instead?"
- Money (leakage): "how many leads come in per week? If even two a month slip, that's $X."
- Money (hard cost): "what does this cost in staff time or external spend?"
- Risk: "what happens when this fails? What's the worst case if it happens again?"
- Reputation: "how fast do you respond right now? Any complaints or reviews tied to this?"

## Create the mechanism
Formula: **"We do (simple process) so that (outcome) happens without (risk/pain)."**
Keep it boringly clear — no tool names, no AI/automation jargon, just what happens and the outcome. Check: "If I run this mechanism, does the outcome naturally happen?" If not obvious, simplify further.

Common B2B mechanism shapes:
- Capture → qualify → route: "We capture leads, qualify them, and route them to the right person with the right context."
- Respond → follow up → book: "We respond fast, follow up automatically, and drive them to a booking."
- Collect → organize → alert: "We collect info, organize it, and alert the team only when action is needed."

**Always pick the choke point closest to the money** — the last step before the lead/sale converts, not a generic upstream issue (e.g. a confusing booking widget beats "no testimonials on the site").

Framing choice: lead with **pain** for messy/broken pathways (creates urgency). Lead with **outcome** for already-polished brands (feels respectful, not accusatory).

## Client inputs (state this to the client directly)
Projects go late because the client doesn't provide what's needed — make it their
stated responsibility. Examples: access to email/calendar/CRM, branding assets, offer
details, FAQs/policies, a team contact for when the decision-maker is unavailable. The
delivery timeline starts when inputs are received — say this to yourself and to them.

## Delivery timeline (give a clear staged timeline, no fluff)
Example structure: Day 0–2 setup → Day 3–7 build + internal test → Day 8–10 client
tweaks → Day 11–14 go live and handover. A clear timeline makes the offer feel real and
prevents "when will this be done" nagging.

## Scope rule of thumb
**If it's not listed as included, it's not part of the scope** — say this to the client
directly. Most price comparison happens when scope is ambiguous; ambiguity invites
negotiation, clarity shuts it down.

## Proof Stack or Proof Proxy
Back the offer with real proof (results, testimonials, case studies) whenever you have
it. Without proof yet, a **proof proxy** is acceptable — a relevant credential, a
demo/prototype, a guarantee, or transferable experience from adjacent work — but it must
be genuine and honestly framed.

**The one hard rule: proof is best, proxies are acceptable, lying is never allowed.**
Never fabricate results, testimonials, or claims.

Other proxy types:
- **Pilot plan proof** — reduce risk with structure: "Running a 14-day pilot, success
  measured by [metric]. If we don't hit baseline improvement, we don't expand scope."
- **Logic proof** — simple cause and effect: "If response times drop from 12 hours to
  10 minutes, conversion rises because fewer leads go uncalled." Decision-makers love logic.

## Pricing, packaging, and the 15-second test
See decision-call.md for the full Pricing Gravity system (operating cost × 3 = floor,
walkaway rule, outcome escalation) and the 15-Second Offer Test — these are the next
steps after the pain/mechanism work above, used once you're in front of a buyer.

## Define The Outcome (full formula)
Businesses buy the before-and-after result, not effort. Outcome must be **measurable and
time-bound**. Avoid vanity outcomes (busywork that sounds nice but isn't tied to a
number or deadline) — without a deadline you sound like a long-term consultant; with one,
the offer feels tangible and creates urgency.

**Formula:** "In [time frame], we go from [before] to [after], measured by [a metric the
buyer already understands]." Good metrics: response time, cost per lead, hours saved,
lead-to-call conversion rate.

**Worked examples (use these time-frame bands as defaults):**
- 7 days (fast setup): "In 7 days, we can cut response times from 12 hours to under 30
  minutes, measured by average first response time."
- 14 days (system build + tuning): "In 14 days, we can reduce admin work from ten hours a
  week to three, measured by staff time tracking." Or: "In 14 days, we can get 100% of
  inbound leads contacted within ten minutes, measured by CRM timestamps."
- 30 days (full rollout, measurable change): "In 30 days, we can increase lead-to-call
  conversion from 8% to 15%, measured by booked calls per lead."

## The Offer — the exact 4-component template
Every real offer has exactly 4 components. Nothing else. Missing even one and the offer
struggles.

1. **Outcome** — "we get you from A to B." One outcome only — don't widen it to fit "lots
   of people," that loses clarity. Buyers buy relief, not complexity.
2. **Mechanism** — "we do this through [simple mechanism in plain English]." No tool
   names this early — keep it feeling like a safe, controllable variable.
3. **Differentiator ("the spine")** — "in order to make that happen, we [X, Y, Z]... this
   works because [why this fixes what keeps breaking elsewhere / what others have failed
   at]." Contrast against the common (failing) approach: "most people try to solve this
   with X — we install [mechanism] that eliminates that delay entirely."
4. **Delivery/scope + next step** — state the boundary ("this runs inside/on top
   of/alongside your existing systems"), state price ("solutions like this typically
   start at $[X] depending on scope"), state the next step calmly: "the next step is we
   secure the investment and begin execution — once secured, setup begins and you'll have
   this live within X days."

**Full template to fill:** "We help [buyer] go from [pain before] to [clear after,
without the pain]. We do this through [simple mechanism]. In order to make that happen,
we [X, Y, Z] — this works because [why it fixes what keeps breaking elsewhere]. This runs
inside/on top of/alongside your existing systems. Solutions like this typically start at
$[price] depending on scope. The next step is we secure the investment and begin
execution — within X days after delivery, we'll do a walkthrough."

**Worked full example:** "We help agencies contact inbound leads in under five seconds
without sacrificing quality for quantity. Most people end up using GPT prompts and basic
automations and hope someone follows up. We do this through an automated triage response
system that qualifies intent immediately and routes them without human delay. This works
because humans are slow and most automations respond too late. This already runs inside
your existing GHL setup and fixes inbound response speed. Solutions like this typically
start at $15,000 depending on scope. The next step is we secure the investment and begin
execution — within X days after delivery, we'll do a walkthrough."

If you can't say your offer this cleanly, fix the offer before the 15-Second Offer Test
(see decision-call.md).

## Worked pricing example — roofing website offer (real numbers, reusable pattern)
Use this as the template for pricing any fixed-scope build for a home-services client.

**Operating cost:**
| Item | Cost |
|---|---|
| Tools (per client, e.g. AI/build tool subscriptions) | ~$190 total |
| Labor: competitor research (1.5h), layout design (2h), build (5h), copy (1.5h), mobile optimization (1h), testing (0.5h), deployment (0.5h) = 12 hours | 12 × $50/hr = $600 |
| **Total operating cost** | **$790** |

**Pricing floor (3x operating cost):** $2,300–$2,400 — the minimum, never go below it.
**Target price:** $2,500+

**Walk-away rules for this offer type:**
1. **Price floor** — if a client consistently negotiates below the floor, walk away
   calmly: "The minimum price for this build is $[floor]. If that doesn't fit your
   budget, I completely understand, but I wouldn't be the right fit for the project."
2. **Scope expansion** — if they ask for extras (SEO, ads management, logo design,
   extra pages, CRM setup) without a price adjustment, walk away or reprice.
3. **Payment commitment** — never start without a payment structure. Standard: 50%
   upfront, 50% on completion. No upfront commitment → walk away.
4. **Required inputs** — client must provide website access, branding assets, service
   info, photos, contact details within 7–10 days, or the project pauses until received.
5. **Decision maker** — only proceed with the person who can approve. If they keep
   deferring to someone absent, disengage until that person is involved.
